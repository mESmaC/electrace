What it does

A buck converter takes a higher DC input and chops it into a lower, steady output — here, anything from 9–18 V down to a clean 5 V at up to 3 A.

Unlike a linear regulator (which burns the extra voltage as heat), a buck switches the input on and off rapidly through an inductor, so very little energy is wasted — about 85% of the input power reaches the load. That efficiency is why it's the default choice when the input is well above the output, or when current is high enough that heat would be a problem.

How it works, part by part

U1IC
The controller. Compares the feedback voltage against an internal reference and rapidly switches its SW pin to hold the output at 5 V. This one chip is the whole "magic" — and you can still see exactly what surrounds it.
L110 µH
The inductor. Stores energy when the switch is on and releases it when off — the core mechanism that lets a buck move power efficiently instead of burning it.
Cin22 µF
Input cap. Supplies the fast current pulses the switcher demands so the input rail doesn't sag.
Cout47 µF
Output cap. Smooths the switched output into clean DC and keeps the control loop stable.
R1 / R2divider
The feedback divider. Divides the output down to the chip's reference — the ratio is literally what sets your output voltage.

Setting the output voltage

The divider R1/R2 sets Vout. With a 0.8 V reference:

Vout = Vref × (1 + R1 / R2) 5.0 V = 0.8 V × (1 + 39k / 10k) Change R1 and the output voltage changes with it. Electrace recomputes this for you and updates the declared spec automatically.
